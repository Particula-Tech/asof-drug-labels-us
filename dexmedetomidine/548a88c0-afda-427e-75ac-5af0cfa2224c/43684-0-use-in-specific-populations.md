# 8 USE IN SPECIFIC POPULATIONS

## 8.1 Pregnancy

Risk Summary

There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of Precedex use in pregnant women.
Dexmedetomidine has been shown to cross the placental barrier in both animal and human published studies.

In animal studies, dexmedetomidine was fetotoxic when administered to pregnant rats during organogenesis at doses equal to the maximum recommended human intravenous dose based on body surface area (see Data).

The limited available information on Precedex use during pregnancy is not sufficient to inform a drug-associated risk of birth defects or miscarriage.
Precedex should be used during pregnancy only if the potential benefits justify the potential risk to the fetus.

The estimated background risk of major birth defects and miscarriage for the indicated population is unknown.
In the U.S. general population, the estimated background risk of major birth defects and miscarriage in clinically recognized pregnancies is 2–4% and 15–20%, respectively.

Clinical Considerations

Disease-associated maternal and/or embryo/fetal risk

Non-obstetric invasive procedures in pregnant women may be required for a variety of conditions and are generally associated with an increased risk of cesarean sections and lower birth weight.
Intubation-stress induced hemodynamic and hormonal changes in pregnant women receiving general anesthesia (for cesarean section or other procedures) can have deleterious effects on the mother and negatively impact the Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity, and Respiration (Apgar) scores in neonates.
Increasing the depth of anesthesia to prevent these changes may cause transient respiratory depression in both the parturient and the neonate and lower neonatal Apgar scores.

Maternal adverse reactions

Literature reports of administration of dexmedetomidine in pregnant women have reported adverse events including hypotension and bradycardia consistent with the known safety profile of dexmedetomidine.

Fetal/Neonatal adverse reactions

Prenatal exposure to dexmedetomidine in pregnant women may lead to some functional impairment at birth requiring interventions such as tactile stimulation and bag-mask ventilation.

Data

Human Data

The literature reports several controlled studies of dexmedetomidine in pregnant women receiving general anesthesia for elective cesarean section, including pregnant women with pre-eclampsia.
Additional controlled studies examined the effect of epidurally or intrathecally administered dexmedetomidine (usually given adjunctively with other anesthetic and analgesic agents) for labor analgesia and anesthesia for elective or emergency cesarean section.
The majority of these mothers had singleton, term pregnancies and were administered dexmedetomidine within the 36th to 40th gestational week.
In these controlled studies, a combined total of 836 parturients and neonates were exposed prenatally to dexmedetomidine.

Fetal/Neonatal Outcomes

The majority of the dexmedetomidine-exposed neonates exhibited good Apgar scores (mean Apgar score ranging from 7–10), normal heart rates, acceptable oxygenation and acid-base parameters.
Apgar scores of neonates born to mothers who received epidural or intrathecal dexmedetomidine combined with ropivacaine or bupivacaine for labor analgesia were comparable to those of neonates from mothers treated with ropivacaine or bupivacaine alone.

Of the 836 dexmedetomidine-exposed neonates identified in these studies, approximately 10% had Apgar scores <7 at 1 minute after delivery; 16 required tactile stimulation and 10 needed bag-mask ventilation for resuscitation upon delivery (none required intubation).

Maternal Outcomes

Perioperative administration of dexmedetomidine in pregnant women receiving general anesthesia for elective cesarean section moderately decreased Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) and heart rate (HR) and reduced the hemodynamic and hormonal response related to the stress of intubation and surgery.
Dexmedetomidine was associated with a longer time to clinical recovery and extubation compared with patients who received remifentanil.

In a study comparing epidural bupivacaine-dexmedetomidine versus bupivacaine-fentanyl for labor analgesia, 12/44 (27.2%) pregnant women who received bupivacaine-dexmedetomidine had a >20% decrease from baseline in MAP and 10/44 (22.7%) developed bradycardia (HR<60).

In one study, the total duration of labor of mothers who received ropivacaine-dexmedetomidine for epidural analgesia was significantly shorter than that of the mothers who received ropivacaine alone (530.4 ± 87.4 minutes vs. 608.2 ± 98.4 minutes, p<0.05).
In a second study, no differences were found in the duration of the first or second stage of labor between women treated with ropivacaine-dexmedetomidine and those treated with ropivacaine alone.

Animal Data

Teratogenic effects were not observed in rats following subcutaneous administration of dexmedetomidine during the period of fetal organogenesis (from gestation day 5 to 16) with doses up to 200 mcg/kg (representing a dose approximately equal to the maximum recommended human intravenous dose based on body surface area) or in rabbits following intravenous administration of dexmedetomidine during the period of fetal organogenesis (from gestation day 6 to 18) with doses up to 96 mcg/kg (representing approximately half the human exposure at the maximum recommended dose based on plasma area under the time-curve comparison).
However, fetal toxicity, as evidenced by increased post-implantation losses and reduced live pups, was observed in rats at a subcutaneous dose of 200 mcg/kg.
The no-effect dose in rats was 20 mcg/kg (representing a dose less than the maximum recommended human intravenous dose based on a body surface area comparison).
In another reproductive toxicity study when dexmedetomidine was administered subcutaneously to pregnant rats at 8 and 32 mcg/kg (representing a dose less than the maximum recommended human intravenous dose based on a body surface area comparison) from gestation day 16 through weaning, lower offspring weights were observed.
Additionally, when offspring of the 32 mcg/kg group were allowed to mate, elevated fetal and embryocidal toxicity and delayed motor development was observed in second generation offspring.

In a study in the pregnant rat, placental transfer of dexmedetomidine was observed when radiolabeled dexmedetomidine was administered subcutaneously.

## 8.2 Lactation

Risk Summary

Dexmedetomidine is excreted in human milk.
Studies assessing the effects of dexmedetomidine in breastfed children and on milk production have not been performed.

Radio-labeled dexmedetomidine administered subcutaneously to lactating female rats was excreted in milk.

The developmental and health benefits of breastfeeding should be considered along with the mother's clinical need for dexmedetomidine and any potential adverse effects on the breastfed child from dexmedetomidine or from the underlying maternal condition.

Clinical Considerations

A lactating woman may consider interrupting breastfeeding and pumping and discarding breast milk for 24 hours after receiving dexmedetomidine in order to minimize potential drug exposure to a breastfed neonate [see Clinical Pharmacology (12.3)].

Data

Human Data

In two published clinical studies, a total of 14 women were given dexmedetomidine IV 6 mcg/kg/hour for 10 minutes after delivery followed by continuous infusion of 0.2–0.7 mcg/kg/hour.
Breast milk and maternal blood samples were collected at 0, 6, 12 and 24 hours after discontinuation of dexmedetomidine.
Plasma and milk dexmedetomidine concentrations were detectable up to 6 hours in most subjects, up to 12 hours in one subject and undetectable in all at 24 hours.
The milk-to-plasma ratio from single paired maternal milk and plasma concentrations at each time point ranged from 0.53 to 0.95.

## 8.4 Pediatric Use

Safety and efficacy have not been established for Procedural or ICU Sedation in pediatric patients.
One assessor-blinded trial in pediatric patients and two open label studies in neonates were conducted to assess efficacy for ICU sedation.
These studies did not meet their primary efficacy endpoints and the safety data submitted were insufficient to fully characterize the safety profile of Precedex for this patient population.
The use of Precedex for procedural sedation in pediatric patients has not been evaluated.

## 8.5 Geriatric Use

Intensive Care Unit Sedation

A total of 729 patients in the clinical studies were 65 years of age and over.
A total of 200 patients were 75 years of age and over.
In patients greater than 65 years of age, a higher incidence of bradycardia and hypotension was observed following administration of Precedex [see Warnings and Precautions (5.2)].
Therefore a dose reduction may be considered in patients over 65 years of age [see Dosage and Administration (2.2) and Clinical Pharmacology (12.3)].

Procedural Sedation

A total of 131 patients in the clinical studies were 65 years of age and over.
A total of 47 patients were 75 years of age and over.
Hypotension occurred in a higher incidence in Precedex-treated patients 65 years or older (72%) and 75 years or older (74%) as compared to patients <65 years (47%).
A reduced loading dose of 0.5 mcg/kg given over 10 minutes is recommended and a reduction in the maintenance infusion should be considered for patients greater than 65 years of age.

## 8.6 Hepatic Impairment

Since Precedex clearance decreases with increasing severity of hepatic impairment, dose reduction should be considered in patients with impaired hepatic function [see Dosage and Administration (2.2) and Clinical Pharmacology (12.3)].
